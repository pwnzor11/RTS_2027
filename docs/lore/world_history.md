## Timeline 1962-2001 (for the Archive overview page)

- **Oct 1962.** The crisis does not end at the brink. In nine days the major cities of the continent are struck; the
  reactors that were meant to power them burn for years afterwards.
- **Nov 1962.** A sealed continuity-of-government complex under a mountain range closes its blast doors from the
  inside. It calls itself the Interior Annex. It is never opened again from outside.
- **Winter 1962-63.** The First Winter. Ash blocks the sun; the grid is gone; county engineers, utility crews and
  railway dispatchers keep towns alive by hand.
- **Feb 1963.** The Lakeshore Conference. Surviving engineers, dispatchers and county clerks sign the Charter of
  Reconstruction. The Continental Reconstruction Authority exists on paper, and a year later in practice.
- **1968.** The first armoured scraper takes ash from a field and a centrifuge turns it into reactor fuel. Ashfall
  becomes the Authority's only scalable energy.
- **1971.** The last public address of the Director, recorded and broadcast from the Annex. After this he is heard
  only through the Voice.
- **1974.** Authority signals units log a steady carrier from the north-west and call it "the hum". Their report is
  filed and, by decision of the administrator's office, left unanswered.
- **1979.** The Convoy Roads are declared open across the Rust Belt. Trade, surveying and census work begin again.
- **1983.** The Annex starts mustering its second generation as "conscripts of the Interior" and opens its first
  surface hatch at night.
- **1985.** The Voice begins the Reclamation broadcasts: the surface is held in trust, and the Directorate will take
  it back.
- **1990.** The Great Drift. A winter of heavy ashfall buries the Glass Coast and exposes old launch sites.
- **1994.** Shaft Seven floods and its reactor fails. Rationing in the Annex turns into the Commissariat's first
  purges.
- **1997.** First sighting of surface hatches by an Authority survey. Reported; again not acted on.
- **Spring 2000.** A survey convoy on the Rust Belt road goes silent. The search party finds a hatch and a loudspeaker.
- **2001.** The Directorate surfaces openly. The Authority mobilises its citizen army. The war begins.

### history_the_exchange
title: The Exchange
subtitle: October 1962
unlock: open

For nine days in October 1962 a standoff that everyone expected to end at the brink did not. Missiles, then
bombers, then the few reserve weapons nobody had counted, fell on cities, rail hubs and reactor sites. The people who
lived through it rarely describe the strikes. They describe the sound of the radio afterwards, when stations went off
the air one by one and then did not come back.

The war never had a surrender. It simply ran out of things to throw. What was left was a continent of fires, broken
power lines and a sky the colour of a bruise.

### history_first_winter
title: The First Winter
subtitle: 1962 to 1963
unlock: open

The ash cloud held the sun back for most of a year. Crops failed, rivers ran grey, and cities that had survived the
strikes froze in the dark. The people who got through it were not soldiers or politicians but the ones who knew how
things worked: lineworkers, water engineers, rail dispatchers, grain-elevator foremen.

They kept lists. Who had a boiler that still ran, who had a truck, who had a doctor. Those lists, copied by hand from
town to town, became the first map of the Authority before it had a name.

### history_the_charter
title: The Charter of Reconstruction
subtitle: How the Authority formed, 1963
unlock: open

In February 1963, delegates from forty-one surviving counties met by lamplight on a frozen lakeshore. They signed a
single page of rules: restore power, restore roads, count the living, and give up emergency powers the day the work
is done. They called it the Continental Reconstruction Authority because nobody could think of a more boring name,
and boring seemed safe.

The final clause, that the Authority must one day dissolve itself, is still read aloud at every swearing-in. Sixty
years on, no one has found a day when the work was done.

### history_the_annex
title: The Interior Annex
subtitle: How the Directorate formed, 1962 to 1971
unlock: open

Under a mountain range, a complex built for a few hundred senior officials and their staff sealed itself at the first
alert. When the Exchange ended there were eleven thousand people inside, many of them the families and clerks nobody
had planned to admit. The Director, the senior surviving official, declared the doors closed "until the surface is fit
to be governed".

The surface was never declared fit. Inside, rationing became law, law became ritual, and the Director's recorded
addresses became the only voice of authority a generation of children ever heard. After 1971 he was no longer seen at
all. The government of the Interior continued, and it continued louder.

### history_ashfall_decades
title: The Ashfall Decades
subtitle: 1963 to 1990
unlock: open

Radioactive ash kept falling for decades, from the dust belt and from reactor cities that smouldered unattended. It
poisoned fields, and it also fuelled the only machines anyone could still build. Scrapers collected it, centrifuges
refined it, and a vehicle with a small reactor in its belly could outlast any fleet of petrol trucks.

The land under the richest drifts became the land that mattered. Pitchblende pits, where the ash had settled onto
exposed ore, were guarded like granaries. A new kind of border appeared: not rivers or ridges, but the edge of a good
field.

### history_first_contact
title: First Contact
subtitle: The slide into war, 1974 to 2001
unlock: open

The Authority heard the Directorate long before it saw it: a carrier signal in 1974, then voices reading orders in
1985, then survey crews reporting hatches in 1997. Each report was filed, and the answer was always the same, that a
reconstruction authority has no mandate to dig up its neighbours.

In spring 2000 a survey convoy vanished on a Rust Belt road. When the Authority sent a search party the next
season, a hatch opened in a field of ash and a loudspeaker told them, politely, that they were trespassing on
reclaimed territory. By the following winter the Directorate had stopped asking.
